# Single-Offer Landing Page

A homepage has to serve every visitor's intent. A landing page only has one job: **one offer, to one audience, toward one action.** Every section either advances that one action or gets cut.

## Discover before designing
If any of this is missing, ask rather than guess:

**The action** — what's the single thing a visitor should do (start trial, join waitlist, download, book a call)? What exactly do they receive for doing it? What counts as success — a click, a form submit, a completed purchase?

**The reader** — who's arriving, what problem brought them here, and what's stopping them from converting today? Where is the traffic coming from (search, an ad, a social post, an email) — that determines how much context they already have when they land.

**What backs the claim** — testimonials, usage numbers, screenshots, a demo clip, guarantee terms. A page with no proof reads as an unverified promise.

**Constraints** — brand tone, visual direction already decided, whether mobile is the primary surface for this traffic.

## The structural skeleton
Above the fold has to work with zero scrolling:
1. Headline (the outcome, tied to who it's for)
2. Subheadline (fills in *how*, adds a detail the headline didn't have room for)
3. One primary call to action
4. One proof signal — a logo row, a stat, a one-line quote
5. A hero visual: real product, not abstract decoration

The middle section carries the argument: the problem framed in the reader's words, three to five benefits stated as outcomes (not feature descriptions), a three-step "how it works," then supporting proof.

The bottom handles what's still unresolved: an FAQ addressing real objections, whatever reduces perceived risk (trial, cancellation terms, refund policy), and a final call to action that mirrors the one at the top.

## Pick a shape before writing copy
- **Hero + stacked sections** — the default, works when the product is legible from a single screenshot.
- **Long-form narrative** — reach for this when the audience needs to be educated or talked out of skepticism before they'll act; more like a sales letter than a page.
- **Minimal single-screen** — for traffic that already knows what this is (a warm list, a referral) and just needs the shortest possible path to the action.
- **Comparison page** — built for search traffic that's actively evaluating alternatives ("X vs Y"); usually one of several SEO-driven pages, not a one-off.

## What actually moves conversion
- **Match the entry point.** If someone clicked an ad, the hero should echo that ad's exact promise — a mismatch here reads as bait-and-switch even when it isn't.
- **One CTA, repeated, not several competing ones.** Multiple different calls to action above the fold force a decision the visitor didn't come to make.
- **State benefits, not capabilities.** A feature is what the product does; a benefit is what changes for the reader because of it.
- **Trade vague claims for a specific number.** "Turns a two-week process into two days" persuades where "makes it faster and easier" doesn't.
- **Put proof next to the claim it backs**, not bundled into a testimonials section at the bottom disconnected from what it's proving.
- **Address the biggest objection early** if the offer is high-friction — burying it in an FAQ nobody scrolls to defeats the point of having one.

## Copy patterns worth reusing
Headline shapes: *"{outcome}, without {the usual pain}"* · *"The {category}, built for {specific audience}"* · *"{result}, in {a surprisingly short time}"*

Subheadline: one to two sentences, naming what it is and who it's for — nothing more.

CTA verbs that commit to something specific beat generic ones: "Start free trial," "Book a demo," "Get the checklist" — never "Learn more" or "Submit."

Benefit bullets pair the outcome with its proof: **bolded outcome** — the specific detail that makes it credible.

## Build it section by section
Work in this order and don't regenerate the whole page for each change: hero → benefits → how it works → proof → FAQ → final CTA. Editing one section at a time keeps every earlier decision intact instead of drifting with each pass.

## Should this page be indexed?
Skip indexing (`noindex`, or keep it off the public sitemap) for ad-only traffic and time-bound offers — a stale promotional page ranking in search after the offer ends does more harm than good.

Index it when the offer is evergreen and matches real search intent: give it a real title and meta description, link to it from elsewhere on the site, and write the FAQ as genuine question-and-answer pairs (this doubles as groundwork for answer-engine visibility, not just traditional SEO).

## Where these pages usually fail
Too many competing calls to action above the fold. Vague value language ("streamline," "optimize") standing in for a real claim. A feature list with no stated outcome attached. Proof pushed to the bottom where nobody scrolls. A mobile layout that breaks the reading order. No unambiguous next step.

## What to hand back when generating one
Page outline (sections, in order) → hero copy (headline, subheadline, CTA, one proof line) → benefits (3–5, outcome-first) → how it works (3 steps) → FAQ (6–12 pairs) → indexing recommendation → which of the four layout shapes, and why.
